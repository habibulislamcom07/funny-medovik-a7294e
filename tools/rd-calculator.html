About the RD Calculator

A recurring deposit is the boring cousin of the mutual fund SIP — and that's exactly why it works. You commit the same amount every month, the bank pays a fixed rate, and you know your maturity figure on day one. Put $300 a month into an RD at 7.1% for five years and you'll end up with about $21,530. Of that, $18,000 is your own money and $3,530 is interest the bank pays you for showing up on time.

The math behind RD is slightly more involved than a lump-sum FD because every deposit earns interest for a different length of time. Your first installment compounds for the full 60 months, the second for 59, and so on. Banks typically compound quarterly, which is why the formula divides the annual rate by 400 instead of 100 — you're applying the rate four times a year to an ever-growing balance.

Consider a real example. In India, SBI currently offers around 6.8% on a 5-year RD for regular customers, while HDFC hovers near 7.0%. Post Office RD sits at 6.7%. If you deposit ₹10,000 a month at 7.0% for 5 years, you invest ₹6 lakh and walk away with roughly ₹7.17 lakh. That ₹1.17 lakh interest is taxable — RD interest is added to your income and taxed at your slab rate, unlike PPF which is tax-free under Section 80C.

In the US, recurring deposits don't exist in the same form. The closest equivalents are bank "holiday club" accounts or automatic savings plans, which typically pay 0.5–3% APY — nowhere near Indian RD rates. A Chase savings account pays 0.01% APY on standard balances, so a 12-month $500/month plan earns less than a dollar in interest. For Americans, a CD ladder or a high-yield savings account at 4.5% (like Marcus or Ally) is a far better match.

The trade-off with RDs is liquidity. If you break an RD before maturity, banks usually cut your rate by 0.5–1% and you lose the compounding benefit on the broken period. SBI charges a 0.50% penalty; HDFC charges 1% on RDs above ₹5 lakh. So an RD only makes sense for money you genuinely won't touch — an emergency fund or a goal with a fixed date (a wedding in 2028, a car down payment in 2027).

One number worth watching: the effective yield. An RD advertised at 7.0% actually delivers about 7.4% on your average balance because each installment compounds for a shorter period but the effective annualized return (on money-weighted basis) is slightly higher than the quoted rate. Our calculator handles this automatically — no need to reverse-engineer bank statements.

Use this tool when comparing RD offers across banks, or when deciding between an RD and a monthly SIP. A SIP in an index fund has historically returned 10–12% over 10-year windows, but with volatility and no guaranteed figure. An RD gives you certainty at a lower rate. Neither is wrong — it depends on whether you can stomach watching your ₹6 lakh dip to ₹5.4 lakh in a bad year.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a recurring deposit and how is it different from an FD?
An FD is a one-time lump sum you lock for a fixed period. An RD is the same idea but you deposit a fixed amount every month — say ₹10,000 on the 5th of each month for 3 years. Both pay a fixed rate and guarantee your maturity figure. The key difference is that in an RD, each installment earns interest for a different duration, so the total interest is lower than if you'd deposited the entire sum upfront. Deposit ₹3.6 lakh as a lump-sum FD at 7% for 3 years and you'll earn about ₹83,000. Do the same amount as an RD spread across 36 months and you'll earn closer to ₹41,000.
How is RD interest calculated?
Banks use quarterly compounding on RDs. The formula is M = P × [((1 + i)^n − 1) / i] × (1 + i), where P is your monthly deposit, i is the quarterly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 400), and n is the number of months in the tenure. That last × (1 + i) accounts for the first month's deposit earning interest starting from month one. If you deposit ₹5,000/month at 6.8% for 24 months, the quarterly rate is 0.017, n is 24, and the formula gives roughly ₹1,28,400 in maturity against ₹1,20,000 invested.
Is RD interest taxable?
Yes, fully taxable in India at your income slab rate. If you're in the 30% bracket, a ₹1 lakh interest payout effectively becomes ₹70,000. Banks deduct 10% TDS if your total interest from that bank exceeds ₹40,000 in a financial year (₹50,000 for senior citizens) — but that TDS is not your final tax, just a prepayment. In the US, "holiday club" account interest is reported on Form 1099-INT and taxed as ordinary income. There's no special exemption for RDs the way PPF or 5-year tax-saver FDs have under Section 80C.
What happens if I miss a monthly installment?
Banks charge a penalty — usually ₹1 to ₹1.50 per ₹100 of the missed installment per month. SBI charges ₹1.50 per ₹100; HDFC charges ₹1.50 for RDs up to ₹5 lakh and ₹2 beyond that. If you miss three consecutive installments, the bank can close the RD and pay you back at the savings-account rate, which is typically 2.7–3%. So a ₹10,000 missed installment at SBI costs you ₹150 that month, and if it drags into default, you lose the entire rate advantage. Set up auto-debit from your savings account the day after salary credit — most banks let you do this in the app.
Can I break an RD early, and what's the penalty?
Yes, but you'll pay a price. Most banks cut your effective rate by 0.5–1% on premature closure. If you opened a 5-year RD at 7.1% and close it after 18 months, the bank will recalculate interest at, say, 6.6% — and only on the months you actually held it. On a ₹10,000/month RD, that penalty eats about ₹500–800 depending on tenure. Some banks refuse premature closure within the first 3 months entirely. Senior citizens and small RDs (under ₹1 lakh) sometimes escape the penalty — check your bank's fine print before assuming.
Is RD a good alternative to a monthly SIP?
They serve different goals. A SIP in an equity index fund has historically returned 10–12% CAGR over 10-year periods, but a bad year can leave you down 15%. An RD gives you 6.5–7.5% with zero volatility and a guaranteed maturity figure. For money you'll need in 1–3 years — a wedding, a car down payment, an emergency buffer — the certainty of an RD beats the upside of a SIP. For money you won't touch for 7+ years, the SIP wins by a wide margin even after a market crash or two. Many people run both: SIP for long-term wealth, RD for short-term goals.
Which banks offer the best RD rates right now?
Rates move quarterly with the RBI repo rate, so check before you commit. As of recent quarters, small finance banks like AU Small Finance (7.25%) and Ujjivan (7.5%) lead on 3-year RDs, followed by HDFC (7.0%), ICICI (7.0%), and SBI (6.8%). Post Office RD sits at 6.7% but is fully government-backed, which matters if you're parking more than ₹5 lakh (DICGC insurance caps at ₹5 lakh per bank per depositor). Senior citizens get 0.25–0.5% extra at most banks — SBI gives 0.50%, HDFC gives 0.25% on select tenures. Don't chase 0.3% differences across banks; the paperwork isn't worth it.
