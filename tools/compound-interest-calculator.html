About the Compound Interest Calculator

Compound interest is one of the most powerful concepts in personal finance, often described as "interest on interest." Unlike simple interest, which is calculated only on the original principal, compound interest is calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. Over time, this compounding effect can dramatically grow your wealth — or your debt — depending on which side of the equation you're on. Our free Compound Interest Calculator helps you visualize exactly how your money grows over time, so you can make smarter investment and savings decisions.

The mathematics behind compound interest is straightforward but the results are astonishing. The standard formula is A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where A is the final amount, P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate (as a decimal), n is the number of times interest is compounded per year, and t is the number of years. For example, if you invest $10,000 at 8% annual interest compounded quarterly for 10 years, the final amount would be approximately $22,080 — more than double your initial investment without adding a single dollar. This is why Albert Einstein reportedly called compound interest "the eighth wonder of the world."

The frequency of compounding makes a significant difference to your returns. Daily compounding yields slightly more than monthly, which in turn beats quarterly, half-yearly, and yearly compounding. This is because interest is added to your balance more often, allowing subsequent interest to be calculated on a larger base. Our calculator supports all five common frequencies — yearly, half-yearly, quarterly, monthly, and daily — so you can compare how each affects your final maturity amount. Banks and financial institutions in different countries use different conventions, so understanding this distinction is important when comparing investment products.

Compound interest is not just for investments — it also works against you on loans and credit card debt. A credit card charging 36% annual interest compounded monthly can turn a $5,000 balance into over $7,000 in just one year if unpaid. This is why financial experts emphasize paying off high-interest debt before investing. On the flip side, long-term investments like retirement accounts, fixed deposits, provident funds, and index funds all benefit from compounding, and the earlier you start, the more dramatic the effect. Starting at age 25 versus age 35 can double your final retirement corpus, even with identical monthly contributions.

It runs entirely in your browser, meaning your financial data never leaves your device. There's no signup, no email required, and no limit on how many calculations you perform. Whether you're planning a fixed deposit, comparing loan offers, or teaching a class about the time value of money, this calculator gives you accurate, instant results.

Understanding compound interest empowers you to ask the right questions: What rate do I need to double my money in 10 years? How much more will I earn if I choose monthly compounding over yearly? Should I pay off my loan early or invest the surplus? These decisions can mean the difference of thousands of dollars over a lifetime. Use this calculator as your starting point, and remember — the best time to start investing was yesterday; the second-best time is today.

Frequently Asked Questions

Everything you need to know about compound interest.

What is compound interest?
Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. Unlike simple interest, which only grows on the principal, compound interest grows exponentially because each period's interest starts earning its own interest. This creates a snowball effect that can dramatically increase your returns over long time horizons.
What is the compound interest formula?
The standard compound interest formula is A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where A is the final maturity amount, P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate in decimal form, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the time in years. For continuous compounding, the formula becomes A = Pert. Our calculator uses the discrete formula with your chosen frequency.
Does compounding frequency really matter?
Yes, but the difference shrinks as frequency increases. For example, $10,000 at 8% for 10 years yields about $21,589 with yearly compounding, $21,911 with quarterly, $22,080 with monthly, and $22,196 with daily. The gap between daily and monthly is tiny, but the gap between yearly and quarterly is significant. Higher frequency always gives slightly more, but with diminishing returns.
How is compound interest different from simple interest?
Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, so it grows linearly. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus accumulated interest, so it grows exponentially. For example, $10,000 at 8% for 10 years earns $8,000 in simple interest but roughly $12,080 in compound interest (quarterly). The longer the period, the bigger the difference becomes.
Can compound interest work against me?
Absolutely. Compound interest is a double-edged sword. On credit card debt, personal loans, or any high-interest borrowing, compounding works against you. A credit card charging 36% annual interest compounded monthly can inflate a $5,000 balance to over $7,100 in just one year without payments. This is why financial experts recommend clearing high-interest debt before making long-term investments.
What is the Rule of 72?
The Rule of 72 is a quick mental shortcut to estimate how long it takes to double your money. Simply divide 72 by your annual interest rate. At 8% interest, money doubles in roughly 9 years (72 ÷ 8). At 6%, it takes about 12 years. The rule is approximate but remarkably accurate for rates between 4% and 15%, making it a handy tool for quick financial estimates.
